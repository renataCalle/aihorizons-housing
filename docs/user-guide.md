# Pencil It: what it is and how to use it

## In one sentence

Pencil It tells a small developer, in under a minute, whether a Pittsburgh lot is worth spending
money on, and it shows city planners which sites are ready for housing and what holds the rest
back.

## The problem it solves

If you build a handful of homes at a time, every lot you look at raises the same questions: What
does the zoning allow? Will I need a hearing? Is the ground a problem? Can the project even pay
for the land? Today, answering them means paying a lawyer, an architect and an engineer, and most
lots turn out not to work anyway.

Pencil It gives you that first read for free, from public data, before you spend anything. It
is a screening tool, not a ruling: it tells you what the zoning code allows as of a given date
and where the risks are, so you know which lots deserve a closer look and which studies to pay
for first.

For the city the problem is the same one, seen from above: housing is slow to build, and it's
hard to tell whether the cause is the zoning rules, the ground, the pipes or the market. Pencil
It scores every lot the same way and explains every point it takes away, so the reasons add up
across a neighbourhood.

## Who it's for

**Small and mid-size developers** doing 3 to 20 homes in Pittsburgh. You know how a pro forma
works, but you're not a zoning lawyer, and you're screening sites between other jobs. You want
to know if one lot is worth pursuing.

**Municipal planners** at City Planning, the County or a housing agency. You want to know which
sites could take new homes, especially starter homes, and which barriers keep the rest from
working: a zoning rule, a hazard, missing infrastructure, or a market that can't pay for
construction.

Housing nonprofits and community development corporations can use it the same way as a
developer, with a filter for public land (land bank, URA and city-owned lots).

## What you can do with it

Most features serve both kinds of user. The two usually start in different places:

- **A developer** starts from one lot: type its ID or address, read the report, decide whether
  to spend money on it.
- **A planner** starts from an area: search a neighbourhood for a building type, switch to
  the Area view to see what holds sites back, then open reports for the lots worth a closer
  look.

### Look up a lot you already have in mind

Type a parcel ID (like `52-H-93`) or a street address into the search box, or just click a lot
on the map. You get a full site report.

### Find lots that fit what you want to build

Describe what you're after in your own words, for example *"3 townhomes in Hazelwood under
$25k"* or *"flat lots near a bus stop, no zoning hearing"*. Pencil It turns your sentence into
search filters, shows them as chips you can remove or edit, and lists the matching lots, best
first. You can also set the filters by hand: building type and number of homes, neighbourhoods,
approval path, land price, lot size, flat lots, near transit, vacant or public land, and hazards
to exclude.

It also shows **near misses**: lots that would match if you relaxed one filter.

### Survey an area (for planners)

Search a neighbourhood for the kind of housing you want more of, for example *"starter homes
in Hazelwood"* or *"duplexes near transit"*. Every lot on the map is coloured by its verdict, so
you see at a glance where housing works and where it doesn't. Sort the list by score, land
price, time to a permit or land headroom, and click any lot for a quick summary: its score, its
approval path, its biggest risk and the most a developer could pay for the land.

Switch the left panel to **Area view** to see the whole search at a glance: how many lots
fall in each band, and **what holds sites back**, the barriers that cost the most points across
the area. In Hazelwood the biggest one isn't zoning or the ground: for most lots, what new
homes sell for doesn't cover the cost of building them and buying the land. You can share the
area view as a link.

To find out **why** a particular lot scores low, open its report. It shows how many points each problem
costs, and a rule-by-rule table shows which zoning rule each building type fails and by how
much. Near misses are the lots that fail by one condition: they're where a rule change or an
infrastructure fix would make the most difference.

### Read the verdict on a lot

Every report opens with a **score from 0 to 100** and a plain label:

- **Fast track** (75+): straightforward, worth moving on.
- **Feasible with conditions** (50–74): workable, with issues to check.
- **High risk** (under 50): serious problems, or the numbers don't work.

The score is made of three parts, and the report shows how many points each one earned:

- **Approval path**: does the building need a hearing, how likely is it to be granted, and how
  long will it take?
- **Site cost**: how much extra will the ground cost you (steep slopes, old mines, flood zones,
  demolition)?
- **Land headroom**: after building costs and a 15% profit, how much can you afford to pay for
  the land, compared with what it's worth?

### See what could kill the deal

A list of problems, worst first: steep slopes, landslide-prone ground, old coal mines, flood
zones, nearby contamination records, no street access, sewer capacity, condemned buildings, tax
liens. Each one says how much it might add to the cost and the schedule, where the information
comes from, and how to resolve it. Anything the data can't tell us is marked **unknown**, never
treated as fine. Checks that came back clean are listed too.

### See what you could build

The report tests five kinds of project on the lot (a single house, a duplex, a triplex,
townhomes, and a small apartment building) and shows two:

- the best one you can build **by right**, with no hearing, and
- the best one that needs **approvals**, with the odds of getting them.

For each: how many homes, the profit margin, and how many months until you could pull a
permit. A **rule-by-rule table** shows exactly which zoning rule each building type passes or
fails, and by how much.

If you searched for a specific building (say, 2 townhomes), the report scores that building
and tells you if something else fits the lot better.

### Know the most you can pay for the land

The report estimates the **maximum land price**: the most you could pay and still make your
target profit, based on recent home sales nearby. It compares that with the lot's assessed
value, so you see at a glance whether a deal is realistic.

### Check the evidence

Every finding has an **Evidence** link. It opens the source behind the claim: the data and its
date, the section of the zoning code in plain language with a link to the code itself, and how
to resolve the issue.

For projects that need a zoning hearing, the evidence shows the **odds of approval**, based on
how Pittsburgh's Zoning Board actually ruled on 202 recent cases, and **similar cases nearby**:
past requests like yours near this lot and whether they were granted.

### Know what to do next

A short, ordered to-do list: free checks first (like a pre-application meeting with City
Planning), then the cheapest study most likely to rule the lot out. Each step says who does it
and roughly what it costs. The idea is to spend a little to learn a lot, and to walk away early
from bad sites.

### Share it

- **Export memo** turns the report into a PDF memo with a list of sources, ready to send to
  a partner or lender.
- Every search and report has its own link, so you can send exactly what you're looking at.

### See the neighbourhood in 3D

The **3D** button tilts the map and raises the surrounding buildings to their real heights, with
each lot coloured by its verdict. It helps you picture what a new building would sit next to.
You can switch between two map styles.

## What it covers today

- **Where:** the City of Pittsburgh. The demo shows Hazelwood: 3,625 lots, of which 1,018 are
  development candidates. Lots outside the city get a partial report that says zoning isn't
  covered there.
- **What it draws on:** 46 public data sources (property records, zoning, slope and mine maps,
  flood maps, environmental records, home sales, transit), 335 rules from the city's zoning
  code, and 202 recent Zoning Board decisions.
- **How it handles uncertainty:** every number is a range (a low, likely and high value),
  because costs, prices and approvals are uncertain. The report says which assumptions are
  measured and which are still estimates.

## What to keep in mind

- **It's a first screen, not advice.** It never says a project is "approved" or "compliant".
  Confirm with the city, a surveyor or a lawyer before you commit.
- **Construction cost is an estimate.** It affects most verdicts. Right now it's why no
  Hazelwood lot reaches "fast track": at today's assumed cost, most projects struggle to pay for
  their land.
- **Zoning Board history is short.** The city's decisions are available online from 2025, so the
  approval odds rest on about 200 cases.
- **Some things aren't in the app yet:** saving sites, map layers for slopes, mines and floods,
  and changing the assumptions (cost, prices, margin) yourself. For planners: comparing chosen
  lots side by side, comparing neighbourhoods with each other, and testing a proposed rule
  change.
